Regional Integration and EU-ACP Trade Correcting for a ratio regressor problem in gravity estimation

Noah Damski  ·  78 ACP countries × EU-27 + UK  ·  2000–2021  ·  42,166 directed dyad-years
85%
of the naive coefficient vanishes once one mechanical channel is frozen

Abstract

A standard measure of regional integration divides a country's trade with its own bloc by its total trade, a denominator that includes trade with the EU, the very outcome the model explains. Because of that overlap, part of any coefficient on this measure is mechanical rather than economic. Holding the EU-trade share of the denominator fixed removes most of it: a large, seemingly robust stumbling-block coefficient (−2.485) falls by 85%, to a number no longer distinguishable from zero. Removing the ratio construction entirely, rather than partially, gives a cleaner test: entering its numerator and denominator as separate variables rejects the equal-and-opposite relationship a ratio assumes, in every version tested. Measured this way, the actual relationship is positive. Regional and EU trade expand together, not apart.

Question

Since the Cotonou Partnership Agreement of 2000, the EU has pushed for both closer EU-ACP trade ties through Economic Partnership Agreements and deeper regional integration among ACP countries themselves. If regional trade grows at the expense of trade with Europe, those two goals work against each other. Most research on this kind of question looks at North-North or North-South agreements; South-South integration's effect on a concurrent North-South relationship gets less attention.

The standard way to measure regional integration is IT Share, the portion of a country's trade that stays inside its own bloc. The first regression using it returned a large, negative, and highly consistent coefficient. IT Share is also a ratio, and its denominator includes trade with the EU, so before treating that number as a real relationship, it needed to be tested against a simpler explanation: arithmetic.

Model

  • Panel. Directed dyad-year, 78 ACP countries by EU-27 plus UK, 2000–2021, 42,166 observations.
  • Treatment. IT Shareit, the share of ACP country i's total trade that stays inside its own REC in year t. It varies by country and year only, not by which EU partner it is matched against.
  • Estimator. PPML, which keeps zero-trade country pairs instead of dropping them and stays consistent under the heteroskedasticity common in trade data (Santos Silva and Tenreyro, 2006).
  • Fixed effects. EU-partner by year, and ACP-country. Errors clustered on the ACP country. ACP-country by year effects are not possible, since IT Share only varies at that level.
  • Sources. BACI (Gaulier and Zignago, 2010), CEPII Gravity (Conte, Cotterlaz and Mayer, 2022), World Bank WDI, EPA dates from EUR-Lex.

Reading the ratio

The problem sits in the denominator of IT Share itself. Total trade includes trade with the EU, so the regressor moves with the outcome by construction, separate from anything economic.

Differences across blocs

Splitting this same baseline coefficient by regional bloc tests whether the effect is the same size everywhere. Six of the seven blocs show a negative coefficient, matching the pooled estimate above. The seventh, Central Africa, is positive, but only at the 10% level (t = 1.72), short of the 5% bar used everywhere else on this poster. A formal test, comparing a model where every bloc gets its own coefficient against one where they all share a single coefficient, confirms the blocs are not identical (F = 2.87, p = 0.014). Mean IT Share itself ranges from 0.044 (ESA) to 0.324 (SADC).

Exports and imports, separately

Testing exports and imports on their own was part of the plan from the start, not an afterthought. The two baseline share coefficients above, −2.591 (ACP exports) and −2.283 (EU exports), are statistically indistinguishable from each other (difference −0.308, conservative SE 1.091).

Same story in logs

Holding the denominator fixed, above, asks whether removing the mechanical channel changes the coefficient. This asks a more basic question: is a ratio even the right way to combine intra-regional and non-EU trade in the first place? Testing the ratio's restriction

A ratio forces a specific relationship. ln(intra-regional trade ÷ non-EU trade) is mathematically the same as ln(intra-regional trade) − ln(non-EU trade). So dividing the two into one variable silently assumes their coefficients are equal in size and opposite in sign, whether or not that is actually true. The test below checks it directly, in eight versions: pooled uses total non-EU trade as the second variable; disjoint split excludes intra-regional trade from that total so nothing overlaps; side-matched pairs import-side components with EU exports and export-side components with ACP exports.

Each pair is one regression, shown in the same units as the −2.485 estimate at far left. If the ratio's restriction held, blue and orange would point in opposite directions. In seven of eight pairs they don't, both point the same way. The one exception, "side-matched, EU exports," is explained below.

The import-side exception

EU exports to ACP are the same flow as ACP imports, so this equation makes more sense measured against ACP's own import-side shares rather than its share of total trade. Even with that adjustment, the ratio version stayed negative: −1.243 (0.257), t = −4.83, significant. Entering import-side intra-regional and import-side non-EU trade as two separate variables instead resolves it: −0.029 (0.024), t = −1.23, not significant. The matching export-side test, for ACP exports to the EU, gives +0.143 (0.043), t = 3.33, positive. The negative reading was coming from the denominator here too, not from the underlying trade relationship.

What the corrected model shows

The "EU component fixed" test on the left still treats IT Share as a ratio: it only freezes one input to that ratio. This test removes the ratio completely. Intra-regional and extra-regional trade enter as two separate variables rather than one divided by the other, the same setup used for the restriction test. That is the full fix, which is why this result is clean and positive throughout, while the EU-fixed numbers were mixed and one stayed significantly negative.

Trade agreements (EPAs)

Economic Partnership Agreements, the EU's own trade deals with individual ACP countries or blocs, are a separate thing from the regional integration (IT Share) measured everywhere else on this poster. This check uses the original share specification, not the corrected model above. EPAs do not appear to change the picture either way: recentering IT Share at its sample mean, the net EPA effect is −0.037 (SE 0.064, t = −0.59), not distinguishable from zero.

Limitations

This is not a causal estimate. Ideally, each ACP country would get its own trend over time, absorbing anything else changing about that country year to year. That isn't possible here: IT Share only varies at the country-year level and would be absorbed entirely by such a fixed effect. Without it, a country growing quickly for unrelated reasons could show both more regional trade and more EU trade at the same time, a general growth confound rather than the relationship being measured. Extra-regional non-EU trade, included as its own regressor, absorbs much of that general growth story, and its own coefficient is positive (+0.286). That means any confounding left over after including it would push the +0.131 result further positive, not flip a true negative into a false one. The direction of the finding is unlikely to be an artifact of this; the exact size should be treated with more caution.

Nor does this claim that no stumbling block exists anywhere. It claims that the share-form evidence for one, the −2.485 coefficient at the top of this poster, is arithmetic rather than economic. A real diversion effect could still exist; this design is not built to detect a small one.
